214	## The gauges (160 columns, identical in every file)
215	
216	Every panel is cured in an autoclave instrumented with 160 gauges. All 160 are logged automatically for every panel, whether or not that panel is ever tested.
217	
218	| Block | Columns | What it reads |
219	|---|---|---|
220	| Oven zone temperature | `cure_temp_z01` … `cure_temp_z40` | Thermocouples distributed through the oven, °C |
221	| Tool pressure | `press_p01` … `press_p32` | Pressure taps under the vacuum bag, kPa |
222	| Resin flow | `resin_flow_f01` … `resin_flow_f32` | Flow-front gauges around the tool, arbitrary units |
223	| Dielectric cure monitor | `dielectric_d01` … `dielectric_d32` | Ion-viscosity probes tracking the cure reaction |
224	| Vacuum line | `vacuum_v01` … `vacuum_v24` | Line pressures at the bag ports, kPa |
225	
226	The gauges are **highly redundant**. They are 160 different views of a much smaller number of underlying process conditions — how hot the part actually got, how fast it got there, how completely the resin flowed, how much contamination was present. Two gauges on opposite sides of the same oven zone tell you nearly the same thing. This redundancy is why a gauge that drops out can be reconstructed from the others, and it is worth keeping in mind when you look at what pre-training does.
227	
228	## The identifier and the target
229	
230	| Column | Definition |
231	|---|---|
232	| `panel_id` | Panel identifier, unique across all four files. The prefix says which file a panel is in: `TR` train, `DV` dev, `TS` test, `UL` unlabelled. |
233	| `strength_mpa` | **Target.** Interlaminar shear strength, MPa. Measured by loading the panel to failure in a press. Present only in the three `labeled_*` files. |
234	
235	Measuring it destroys the panel. That is the whole problem: a labelled row costs a saleable part plus about four hours of lab time, and an unlabelled row costs nothing at all.
236	
237	**A note on negative values.** About 1–2% of labelled panels show a strength below zero, which no real panel can have. Leave them in: they are the badly delaminated panels, and the prescribed models and every comparison in the brief are computed with them included.

239	## What Process Engineering says about strength
240	
241	> Strength needs two things to go right at once. The resin has to flow far enough to wet out every ply, and the cure has to run far enough to convert the matrix. A panel that is beautifully wetted but under-cured is weak. A panel that is fully cured but resin-starved is weak in a different way. **You cannot make up for a deficit in one with a surplus in the other** — the two failure modes multiply rather than add.
242	>
243	> Separately, contamination above a threshold causes delamination. Below the threshold it does nothing measurable; above it, strength falls away sharply.
244	
245	That paragraph is a description of the physics, not a hint about modelling. It is here because it is what the process engineers would tell you, and it is relevant to at least one thing you will observe.

280	The broad goal of these assignments is to help you become comfortable working with AI to define and implement machine learning pipelines in a business context. To do that, you need a certain level of competency in the core concepts of machine learning. And imparting that competency is the broad goal of the course.

283	finish an assignment and could not explain your headline number on paper, it has not done its job.

288	
289	A business situation, a dataset, and **an analysis someone else has already done** — an analyst, a
290	contractor, an engineer. That analysis is plausible, arithmetically correct, but also wrong. Your task is
291	not "build a model." It is "understand what is wrong, why it is wrong, establish what is true, and say what should be done."
292

296	|---|---|

298	| `analysis/` | Your code. Runs end to end from one command. |

304	## 3. A working order that tends to go well
305	
306	**Read the brief and the data dictionary yourself first.** The dictionaries record how each column
307	is produced and *when*. That timing information decides which columns you may use, and it is easier
308	to take in before a model exists to distract you.
309	
310	**Write down what you expect before you compute anything.** What is the base rate? What order of
311	magnitude should the effect be? What would a suspiciously good result look like? Two minutes of this
312	is what makes you able to be surprised, and being surprised is how you find things.
313	
314	**Get a baseline before you get a model.** Every assignment has a do-nothing answer — predict the
315	majority class, predict the mean, keep the current price. Compute it. It is the denominator for
316	everything that follows and the number most often missing from an agent's output.
317	
318	**Change one thing at a time.** Where an assignment asks for an audit trail — one row per problem,
319	one fix, and the resulting number — build it as you go rather than reconstructing it at the end. It
320	is also your best defence against an agent that quietly fixed three things at once.
321	
322	---
323	
324	## 4. Working with your agent
325	
326	The assignments are graded lightly, and in principle, you can delegate the majority of work to AI. However, this goes against the course's learning goals and will make you struggle in the in-person assessments. Hence, the following strategy is recommended for optimal learning.
327	
328	**Delegate the work, not the judgment.** Good to hand over: "write a grouped cross-validation split
329	by member_id", "sweep the ridge penalty over the grid and plot train and validation error". Not good
330	to hand over: "tell me whether this model is good enough to deploy", "write the recommendation."
331	The second list is the assignment.
332	
333	**Give it the business context, not just the file.** An agent told "predict churn from this CSV" will
334	optimise accuracy. An agent told what an offer costs and what a retained member is worth will do
335	something quite different. The context is in the brief; pass it along.
336	
337	**Ask it to argue against itself.** "What would have to be true for this estimate to be wrong?" and
338	"What is the strongest objection to this conclusion?" are the two highest-yield prompts in the course.
339	
340	**Be suspicious of agreement.** If the first answer confirms what you expected and nothing errored,
341	slow down rather than speed up.
342	
343	**Do not let it choose the evaluation design.** Splits, metrics, units of analysis, and what counts as
344	held out are the graded content of this course. An agent will pick a sensible default, and these
345	datasets are built to punish sensible defaults.
346	
347	**If you do not understand what it produced, stop.** Get it explained or throw it out. You are
348	responsible for every line and may be asked in class to explain any of it.
349	
350	**Export your session before you close it** (`03_exporting_your_session_history.md`). The Codex app
351	copies to the clipboard rather than to a file, so paste it somewhere immediately.
352	
353	---

355	## 5. The memo
356	
357	Written to a named person who has a decision to make and will read the first paragraph carefully and
358	the rest quickly.
359	
360	- **Lead with the recommendation.** Not the method, not the data cleaning.
361	- **Give the number, then the number it replaces.** "The honest estimate is X, not the Y in the
362	  current proposal, and the gap is mostly Z."
363	- **Quantify the uncertainty once, plainly, and move on.** A memo that never commits is not useful.
364	- **State conditions so someone could check them.** "Deploy if corporate accounts stay under 15% of
365	  the book" is a recommendation. "Deploy with caution" is not.
366	- **Respect the length limit.** Exhibits do not count against prose length; prose does.

def _standardizer():
    """Centre and scale using the TRAINING gauges only.  Computed once."""
    global _STATS
    if _STATS is None:
        raw = pd.read_csv(DATA / FILES["train"])
        G = raw.drop(columns=[ID, TARGET]).to_numpy(float)
        _STATS = (G.mean(0), G.std(0) + 1e-9)

def load(which):
    """load('train') -> (X, y).   load('traces') -> X.

    X is always standardised with the training-set statistics, so every matrix

def ridge(Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte, seed=0):
    """Ridge regression.  Penalty chosen on a 75/25 split of the labels."""
    Xtr, Xte = np.asarray(Xtr, float), np.asarray(Xte, float)
    A = np.hstack([Xtr, np.ones((len(Xtr), 1))])
    B = np.hstack([Xte, np.ones((len(Xte), 1))])
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    o = rng.permutation(len(A))
    cut = max(12, int(0.75 * len(A)))
    fit, val = o[:cut], o[cut:]
    I = np.eye(A.shape[1])
    best, best_a = np.inf, RIDGE_ALPHAS[0]
    for a in RIDGE_ALPHAS:
        W = np.linalg.solve(A[fit].T @ A[fit] + a * I, A[fit].T @ ytr[fit])
        e = np.mean((A[val] @ W - ytr[val]) ** 2)
        if e < best:
            best, best_a = e, a
    W = np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + best_a * I, A.T @ ytr)
    return float(np.mean((B @ W - yte) ** 2))


def pretrain(pool, seed=0, epochs=PRETRAIN_EPOCHS, mask_fraction=MASK_FRACTION):
    """Self-supervised pre-training: hide gauges, rebuild them.

    No labels are used or needed.  A fresh set of gauges is hidden at every
    epoch -- that re-drawing is what forces the network to learn the process
    rather than memorise one corruption.

    Returns the encoder.  Pre-train ONCE per pool and reuse it; it is by far
    the most expensive thing here (~10 seconds) and nothing about it depends on
    the labels.
    """
    pool = np.asarray(pool, float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    net = MLP([pool.shape[1], *ENCODER_SHAPE, pool.shape[1]], seed=seed)
    for ep in range(epochs):
        corrupted = pool.copy()
        corrupted[rng.random(pool.shape) < mask_fraction] = 0.0
        net.fit(corrupted, pool, epochs=1, batch_size=PRETRAIN_BATCH,
                lr=PRETRAIN_LR, seed=seed * 1000 + ep)
    return net


def random_encoder(seed=99):
    """An encoder of identical shape whose weights were never trained.

    Handout 9 S3.3: without this you cannot tell 'pre-training learned
    something' from 'this architecture makes useful random features'.
    """
    n = len(gauge_names())
    return MLP([n, *ENCODER_SHAPE, n], seed=seed)

    """The learned features: the encoder's first hidden layer, 64 numbers.

    160 gauges in, 64 learned features out.  Handout 9 S3.4 is about which
    layer to take; the brief fixes it at the first so that everyone's table
    is comparable.
    """
    return enc.hidden(np.asarray(X, float), layer=1)

    """Freeze the encoder, fit ridge on its representation.  Handout 9 S2.1."""
    return ridge(representation(enc, Xtr), ytr,
                 representation(enc, Xte), yte, seed=seed)


def supervised(Xtr, ytr, Xte, yte, seed=0, encoder=None):
    """Train a network end to end and return its test MSE.

    encoder=None  -> from scratch, at SCRATCH_LR.
    encoder=enc   -> fine-tuning: start from the pre-trained weights and train
                     everything at the reduced FINETUNE_LR.

    The target is centred before fitting and the mean added back afterwards.
    Without that the output layer starts far from the data and, with only 50
    labels and early stopping, never gets there.
    """
    Xtr, Xte = np.asarray(Xtr, float), np.asarray(Xte, float)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    o = rng.permutation(len(Xtr))
    cut = max(12, int((1 - VAL_FRACTION) * len(Xtr)))

    net.fit(Xtr[fit], ytr[fit] - centre, epochs=MAX_EPOCHS, batch_size=BATCH,

            X_val=Xtr[val], Y_val=ytr[val] - centre, patience=PATIENCE)
    return float(np.mean((net.predict(Xte).ravel() + centre - yte) ** 2))

**The setup.** Kestrel has 2,000 tested panels. The question is how each method would do if Kestrel had only 50, 100, 250 or 1,000 of them. So we pretend we have fewer labels than we do, at each of those four **label counts**. Every model is always scored on the same 1,000 dev panels. Only the training set changes.

**What a "label draw" is.** Say n = 50. There are many different sets of 50 panels you could pick out of the 2,000, and the answer depends on which 50 you get. One run would mix up how good the method is with how lucky the sample was. So at each n you take **5 different random subsets** of size n and fit on each one. Each subset is a "draw" (also called a replicate).

`label_subset(n, replicate)` in [kestrel.py:96-107](starter/kestrel.py#L96-L107) does this. For replicate r = 0…4 it returns the row indices of n training panels. The random seed is fixed at `9000 + 100*r + n`, so every student gets exactly the same panels and the table is comparable across submissions.

One detail: draws at different n are **not nested**. The seed depends on n, so draw 0 at n = 100 does not contain draw 0 at n = 50. Each label count is a fresh sample.

**The second source of randomness: pre-training seeds.** This one has nothing to do with labels. The encoder is trained on the 20,000 unlabelled traces, and two pre-training runs with different seeds give different encoders. The brief has you train **3 encoders once, before any loops**, and reuse them everywhere. Pre-training never sees a label, so retraining it inside the loop would just waste about 10 seconds per call.

**How the runs fill each cell.** A cell is one method at one n. Its mean ± SD is taken over these runs:

| Method | Runs | Which runs |
|---|---|---|
| Ridge, raw gauges | 5 | draws 0–4 |
| Frozen pre-trained + ridge | 15 | 3 encoders × 5 draws |
| Frozen random + ridge | 5 | 1 untrained encoder × 5 draws |
| From scratch | 5 | draws 0–4 |
| Fine-tuned | 5 | draw r starts from encoder r mod 3 (encoders 0,1,2,0,1) |

The frozen row gets 15 runs because it carries both kinds of randomness. That way its SD shows both "which panels" and "which encoder."

Altogether: 3 pre-trainings, then 4 label counts × 35 fits = 140 fits.

**What "seed=replicate" means.** It is a separate thing from the draw. Inside each fit there is a small internal split:
- **Ridge** keeps 25% of its n labels aside to choose the penalty.
- **The networks** hold out 25% for early stopping, and the seed also sets the starting weights.

`seed=replicate` controls that internal randomness. The draw decides which panels you get, and the seed decides how they are split and initialised. One thing to notice: at n = 50, a network trains on only 37 panels and early-stops on 13.

**Why the SDs matter.** The brief warns that at n = 50 the spread across runs is larger than some of the differences between methods. A gap smaller than the SD can't be read as a real difference. That's why the crossover question asks for a bracket ("somewhere between 100 and 250"), not a point.

"""
A small multilayer perceptron in numpy, for Assignment 2.

You should not need to open this file. `kestrel.py` wraps everything the
assignment asks for. It is here so that nothing in the pipeline is a black box
you could not inspect if you wanted to.

    MLP(sizes)              e.g. MLP([160, 64, 1]) -- one hidden layer of 64
    .fit(X, Y, ...)         Adam, mini-batch, optional early stopping
    .predict(X)
    .hidden(X, layer=1)     the activations of a hidden layer -- the learned
                            representation, which is what gets frozen and reused
    .copy_body_from(other)  copy all layers except the last, which is how a
                            fine-tuned network is initialised from an encoder

Everything is float64 numpy. No GPU, no autograd; the backward pass is written
out. You are not responsible for it. You ARE responsible for knowing what the
model consumes, produces, and assumes.
"""
import numpy as np


def _relu(z):
    return np.maximum(z, 0.0)


class MLP:
    def __init__(self, sizes, seed=0):
        """sizes = [n_in, n_hidden_1, ..., n_out]."""
        self.sizes = list(sizes)
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        self.W, self.b = [], []
        for a, b in zip(self.sizes[:-1], self.sizes[1:]):
            # He initialisation
            self.W.append(rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(2.0 / a), (a, b)))
            self.b.append(np.zeros(b))

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------ core
    def _forward(self, X):
        acts = [X]
        h = X
        for i, (W, b) in enumerate(zip(self.W, self.b)):
            z = h @ W + b
            h = z if i == len(self.W) - 1 else _relu(z)   # linear output layer
            acts.append(h)
        return acts

    def predict(self, X):
        return self._forward(np.asarray(X, dtype=float))[-1]

    def hidden(self, X, layer=1):
        """Activations after `layer` hidden layers.  layer=1 is the first.

        This is the learned representation. Handout 9 S3.4 is about which layer
        to take; Assignment 2 fixes it at the first so that every submission's
        table is comparable.
        """
        return self._forward(np.asarray(X, dtype=float))[layer]

    def copy_body_from(self, other):
        """Copy every layer except the last.  Shapes must match.

        This is what turns a pre-trained encoder into the starting point for a
        fine-tuned predictor: the body carries over, the output layer is new.
        """
        n = len(self.W) - 1
        for i in range(n):
            self.W[i] = other.W[i].copy()
            self.b[i] = other.b[i].copy()
        return self

    # ------------------------------------------------------------------- fit
    def fit(self, X, Y, epochs=200, batch_size=64, lr=1e-3, seed=0,
            X_val=None, Y_val=None, patience=None, verbose=False):
        """Mini-batch Adam on mean squared error.

        If X_val/Y_val and `patience` are given, stops when validation MSE has
        not improved for `patience` epochs and restores the best weights.
        Returns a history dict.
        """
        X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
        Y = np.asarray(Y, dtype=float)
        if Y.ndim == 1:
            Y = Y[:, None]
        rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
        n = len(X)

        mW = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in self.W]
        vW = [np.zeros_like(w) for w in self.W]
        mb = [np.zeros_like(b) for b in self.b]
        vb = [np.zeros_like(b) for b in self.b]
        b1, b2, eps, step = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8, 0

        hist = {"train": [], "val": []}
        best, best_epoch, best_w = np.inf, 0, None

        for ep in range(epochs):
            order = rng.permutation(n)
            for s in range(0, n, batch_size):
                idx = order[s:s + batch_size]
                xb, yb = X[idx], Y[idx]
                acts = self._forward(xb)
                delta = 2.0 * (acts[-1] - yb) / len(idx)
                step += 1
                for i in range(len(self.W) - 1, -1, -1):
                    gW = acts[i].T @ delta
                    gb = delta.sum(axis=0)
                    if i > 0:
                        delta = (delta @ self.W[i].T) * (acts[i] > 0)
                    for g, m, v, p in ((gW, mW, vW, self.W), (gb, mb, vb, self.b)):
                        m[i] = b1 * m[i] + (1 - b1) * g
                        v[i] = b2 * v[i] + (1 - b2) * g * g
                        mh = m[i] / (1 - b1 ** step)
                        vh = v[i] / (1 - b2 ** step)
                        p[i] -= lr * mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + eps)

            tr = float(np.mean((self.predict(X) - Y) ** 2))
            hist["train"].append(tr)
            if X_val is not None:
                Yv = np.asarray(Y_val, dtype=float)
                if Yv.ndim == 1:
                    Yv = Yv[:, None]
                va = float(np.mean((self.predict(X_val) - Yv) ** 2))
                hist["val"].append(va)
                if va < best - 1e-9:
                    best, best_epoch = va, ep
                    best_w = ([w.copy() for w in self.W], [b.copy() for b in self.b])
                elif patience is not None and ep - best_epoch >= patience:
                    if verbose:
                        print(f"early stop at epoch {ep}, best {best:.4f} @ {best_epoch}")
                    break
            if verbose and ep % 20 == 0:
                print(f"epoch {ep:4d}  train {tr:10.4f}"
                      + (f"  val {hist['val'][-1]:10.4f}" if hist["val"] else ""))

        if patience is not None and best_w is not None:
            self.W, self.b = best_w
        hist["best_epoch"] = best_epoch
        return hist

**Not from the 1,000 dev panels.** They come from the **2,000 train panels**. Each file has one job:

| File | Rows | Role |
|---|---|---|

## Q2: What are pre-training and the encoder?

### The problem pre-training solves

Each panel has 160 gauge readings. The data dictionary says these are **redundant**: they are 160 views of a few underlying conditions (how hot the part got, how fast it heated, how well the resin flowed, how much contamination there was). Two thermocouples in the same oven zone say almost the same thing.

With only 50 labelled panels, learning from 160 raw numbers is hard. It would help to first compress each panel into a smaller, more meaningful description. You'd want the description to capture something like "this panel ran hot and resin-starved" instead of listing 160 raw readings.

The key point: **learning that description needs no strength labels.** It only needs to learn how the gauges relate to each other, and there are 20,000 free traces for that.

### Pre-training: a fill-in-the-blanks game

Pre-training builds this network ([kestrel.py:132-151](starter/kestrel.py#L132-L151)):

```
 160 gauges ──► 64 ──► 16 ──► 160 gauges
 (30% blanked)   │                (all of them, the originals)
                 └─ this layer = "the representation"
```

The training loop, using the 20,000 unlabelled traces:
1. Take a panel's 160 gauges and blank out a random 30% of them (set to 0, which is the average reading after standardising).
2. Feed the damaged version in, and ask the network to output **all 160 original values**.
3. Score it on how well it rebuilt the readings (squared error), and adjust the weights.
4. Repeat for 40 passes over the data, blanking a **different** random 30% each time. That way it can't memorise one pattern of gaps.

The "label" here is the data itself, which is why this is called **self-supervised**. Strength is never used.

**Why this teaches the network anything useful.** To guess a blanked thermocouple, it has to work out from the other gauges what the oven was doing. To do that across many panels, it has to learn the underlying conditions. The squeeze through 64 and then 16 numbers makes this worse for it: it cannot copy 160 values through, so it has to compress.

### The encoder

After pre-training, keep the first part (**160 → 64**) and discard the rest (64 → 16 → 160), which only existed to make the game possible. The part you keep is the **encoder**. For any panel it turns 160 gauges into **64 numbers**, and those 64 numbers are the **representation** (`representation()` at [kestrel.py:165](starter/kestrel.py#L165)).

The bet is that these 64 numbers describe the panel's process conditions better than the 160 raw gauges, so a small number of labels goes further.

Training three encoders with seeds 0, 1, 2 gives three somewhat different encoders: same game, different random starts and blanking patterns. That's the second source of randomness from before.

## Q3: Which steps use labelled vs unlabelled data?

| Method | 20k unlabelled? | n labelled? | What gets learned from the n labels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Ridge on raw gauges | no | yes | 160 linear weights on the gauges |
| 2. Frozen encoder + ridge | yes (encoder, once) | yes | Only ridge weights on the 64 features. The encoder is **frozen**, so its weights don't change. |
| 3. Frozen **random** encoder + ridge | no | yes | Same as 2, but the encoder **never played the game**. Its weights are random. |
| 4. Network from scratch | no | yes | A whole 160 → 64 → 1 network, starting from random weights |
| 5. Fine-tuned | yes (starting point) | yes | The same 160 → 64 → 1 network, but its 160 → 64 layer **starts** from the pre-trained encoder, then all weights keep training on the labels at a smaller learning rate |

A few things to notice:
- **Methods 4 and 5 have the same shape**, and the 160 → 64 layer matches the encoder. That's what allows the transplant: `copy_body_from` copies every layer except the output layer. So 4 vs 5 asks whether starting from pre-trained weights beats starting from random ones.
- **Method 3 is the control.** A random 160 → 64 layer with a ReLU still produces 64 nonlinear features, and those might help ridge on their own. Without row 3, if row 2 beats row 1 you couldn't tell whether pre-training learned something or the architecture alone helped (Handout 9 §3.3). Row 2 vs row 3 isolates what the fill-in-the-blanks game added.
- **Frozen vs fine-tuned.** Freezing learns very few things from the labels (65 ridge coefficients), which is safe when labels are scarce. Fine-tuning lets the encoder adjust toward predicting strength, which is more flexible but has more weights to fit from few labels. The brief's rule of thumb (freeze at tens of labels, fine-tune at hundreds to thousands, train from scratch at tens of thousands) is about this trade-off.

## The numbers: 2,000, 1,000 and 50

Each of these numbers means something different:

| Number | What it is |
|---|---|
| **2,000** | All the labelled panels Kestrel actually has, in `labeled_train.csv`. In the story, this is the five years of destructive tests. |
| **50, 100, 250, 1,000** | The **label counts n**. Each is a pretend budget: "what if Kestrel had tested only n panels?" Each is a random subset of the 2,000. |
| **1,000 (dev)** | A separate file of 1,000 extra tested panels, used only to **score** models. |
| **1,000 (test)** | Another 1,000 panels, scored once at the end. |

So "1,000" shows up twice by coincidence. The largest label count is n = 1,000, and the dev file also happens to have 1,000 rows. They are unrelated: a model trained on n = 1,000 train panels is scored on the 1,000 dev panels.

The files contain 4,000 labelled panels in total. Only the 2,000 in train count as Kestrel's labels. Dev and test exist so we can grade models fairly, on panels they never saw.

The table stops at n = 1,000, but Kestrel has 2,000. Keep that in mind for the memo's question about the method to deploy at Kestrel's current label count.

## Your answer about the incumbent

Part of it is on the right track, and part of it is the exact trap the rubric names. Here is why.

**"1,000 is not enough data for a linear model."** For a linear model, 1,000 rows is a lot. Ridge fits 161 numbers (160 weights plus an intercept), so that's about 6 rows per coefficient, and ridge's penalty handles the rest.

Shortage is a real issue at n = 50, where there are fewer rows than coefficients. At 1,000 it's much less believable. The rubric warns against this diagnosis directly: *"Diagnosing the incumbent as short of data."*

**"Collinearity."** The gauges are highly collinear, but ridge is built for exactly that. The penalty spreads weight across redundant gauges instead of letting the fit blow up. Collinearity makes the individual weights hard to interpret. It doesn't make the predictions bad.

**"Non-linear."** This is the promising part. Look at what the data dictionary says about the physics:
- **The failure modes multiply.** Weak resin flow and weak cure combine like "flow × cure." A surplus in one can't make up for a deficit in the other.
- **Contamination has a threshold.** Below it, nothing happens. Above it, strength falls sharply.

A linear model can only produce *weighted sums*. Every gauge pushes strength up or down by a fixed amount, regardless of the other gauges. So it can't express "high cure helps only if flow is also high," or "contamination matters only past a threshold."

## Why this matters: two kinds of limit

This is the bias–variance distinction from Lecture 3:

| | Data shortage (variance) | Wrong model shape (bias) |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Too few rows to pin down the weights | The model *can't express* the true relationship |
| What more data does | Error keeps falling | Error **plateaus**. Even with the perfect weights, a weighted sum is still the wrong shape. |
| Fix | More tests | A different model or features, not more tests |

The engineer is betting on the left column ("another 2,000 tests and re-fit"). If the right column is the true story, his $8.8M buys almost nothing.

That's the diagnosis to settle from the table, not to assert in advance. When it's built, three comparisons will tell you which column applies:
1. **Ridge at n = 50 vs n = 1,000.** Does the error keep dropping, or flatten out?
2. **Ridge vs the do-nothing baseline** (predict the mean strength). How much of the variation does ridge explain at all?
3. **Ridge vs a network at the same n.** If a network does clearly better on the *same* panels, the information is in the gauges and the linear shape can't use it. That points to bias, not shortage.

Before we run anything, write your expectations down in a sentence or two. For example: "I expect ridge to improve only slightly between 50 and 1,000 and to stall well above zero, because…". That's the course's "write it down first" step, and it gives you something to be surprised by.

Your picture of the frozen row is right. The "draws" are not a step after that: they decide **which** 50 (or 100, …) panels you fit on in the first place.

## What a draw is

Kestrel has 2,000 labelled panels. At n = 50 there are many ways to pick 50 of them, and the result depends on which 50 you pick. So we pick **5 different sets of 50**. Each set is one **draw**, numbered r = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4.

```
2,000 train panels
   ├── draw 0: panels #17, #402, #1288, ... (50 of them)
   ├── draw 1: a different 50
   ├── draw 2: a different 50
   ├── draw 3: a different 50
   └── draw 4: a different 50
```

`label_subset(50, r)` returns those 50 row numbers. The same happens at 100, 250 and 1,000: five different random subsets at each size.

The key rule: **within a draw, all five methods train on exactly the same panels.** Any difference between methods inside a draw is then down to the method, not to luck in which panels they saw.

## One pass of the loop: n = 50, draw 0

1. Get the 50 panels: their gauges (50 × 160) and their strengths (50 numbers).
2. Fit each method on those 50 and score it on all 1,000 dev panels:

| Method | What happens with the 50 panels | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Raw ridge | Ridge maps the 160 gauges to strength | 1 |
| Frozen pre-trained + ridge | Pass the 50 panels through **encoder 0**'s first hidden layer to get 64 numbers each, fit ridge on those, and score. Repeat with **encoder 1** and **encoder 2**. | **3** |
| Frozen random + ridge | Same as above, but through the never-trained encoder | 1 |
| From scratch | Train a 160 → 64 → 1 network from random weights (37 to train, 13 to decide when to stop) | 1 |
| Fine-tuned | Same network, but its first layer starts as a copy of encoder r mod 3, here encoder 0 | 1 |

That's **7 dev scores** for this draw.

3. Move on to draw 1, which is a new set of 50 panels, and repeat. Fine-tuning now starts from encoder 1, draw 2 from encoder 2, draw 3 from encoder 0 again, and so on.

## Putting the cell together

After 5 draws at n = 50, each method's cell holds:

| Method | Scores in the cell |
|---|---|
| Raw ridge | 5 (one per draw) |
| Frozen pre-trained | **15** (5 draws × 3 encoders) |
| Frozen random | 5 |
| From scratch | 5 |
| Fine-tuned | 5 (encoders used: 0, 1, 2, 0, 1) |

The table cell is the **mean ± SD** of those scores. Then the whole thing repeats for n = 100, 250 and 1,000.

As code, the whole table is:

```
encoders = [pretrain(traces, seed=s) for s in 0, 1, 2]   # once, before the loops
rand_enc = random_encoder()

for n in 50, 100, 250, 1000:
    for r in 0..4:                       # the 5 draws
        idx = label_subset(n, r)         # which n panels
        X, y = train gauges[idx], train strength[idx]
        fit all five methods on (X, y), score each on dev
```

## What the SD in each cell means

Each SD measures how much the result moves when you change things that **shouldn't matter**:
- **Which panels happened to get tested** (the draw), for every row.
- **Which pre-training run you got** (the encoder), for the frozen row, and partly for the fine-tuned row.

If two methods' means differ by less than that spread, the table can't tell them apart. That's why the brief insists on the draws.

It's the error you'd get by **ignoring the gauges completely**.

## Where it comes from

1. The average strength of the 2,000 train panels is **32.8 MPa**.
2. Use that as the "prediction" for every one of the 1,000 dev panels: "every panel is 32.8 MPa."
3. For each dev panel, take the miss (actual minus 32.8), square it, and average over all 1,000.

That average squared miss is **188.9 MPa²**. It's the **MSE** (mean squared error), the same score every model in the table gets.

## How to read it

- **The units are squared** (MPa²), which makes the number hard to picture. Its square root, **13.7 MPa**, is the typical miss. Strength is about 32.8 ± 13.7 MPa, so guessing the average is off by around 14 MPa on a typical panel.
- **It's the variance of strength on dev.** Predicting the mean leaves all of the panel-to-panel variation unexplained, so the MSE equals the variance (188.8). The extra 0.1 is because the train mean differs very slightly from the dev mean.

## Why it matters

It's the floor for "learned nothing." Any model that uses the gauges should beat it:
- **Near 188.9**: the model barely used the gauges.
- **Halfway to 0, around 94**: the model explained about half the variation in strength (R² ≈ 0.5).
- **Near 0**: almost perfect.

For any model, **1 − MSE / 188.9** is the share of strength variation it explains. That's often the easiest way to say "how good" to someone who isn't a modeller.

**(a) n = 50, "near 188.9."** Reasonable, but think about *why* it can't go much past it. Ridge with 160 gauges and 50 panels has more coefficients than rows. The penalty handles that by shrinking all the weights toward zero, and a ridge with weights near zero just predicts the mean. So the penalty works like a safety net: ridge tends to fall back toward 188.9 instead of doing much worse.

The catch is that it picks the penalty strength on only 13 held-out panels. That choice is noisy, so expect a **large SD** at n = 50. Some draws could even land above 188.9.

Mostly right. One argument points the other way from how you used it, and one word is too strong.

**"Linear in the representation."** Correct, and it's the core idea. Ridge is still drawing a straight-line relationship, but over 64 *new* features. Each feature is a ReLU of a weighted mix of gauges, so a nonlinear function of the gauges. Ridge on those features can therefore express some curved shapes in terms of the original gauges, which raw ridge can't.

**"Fewer coefficients."** True (65 vs 161), but look at *where* that helps. Fewer things to estimate means less variance, and variance is the problem when labels are scarce. That argues for a **bigger** benefit at **n = 50**, not n = 1,000. At 1,000, estimating 161 coefficients is easy anyway.

So you've listed two separate reasons, and each matters at a different end:
- **Compression** (fewer, more meaningful features) helps most at **small n**.
- **Better shape** (nonlinear features) helps at **every n**, and it's the only one still helping at **large n**, once variance is no longer the issue.

That's worth keeping, because the table can show which one is doing the work.

**"64 is still bigger than 50."** Raw ridge already handled 160 features with 50 rows, so the feature count alone isn't the barrier. What matters is whether the strength signal **lies in a few directions** of the feature space. If the encoder really captured the handful of underlying conditions (heat, flow, cure, contamination), the signal is concentrated and ridge can find it from few labels. Handout 9 §3.1 says this is exactly where pre-training is supposed to pay most: where labels are scarcest.

They're two separate things that work together. **Adam decides how each weight update is made. Early stopping decides when to stop making updates.** Early stopping is not part of Adam.

## Adam: how each step is taken

Training a network is a loop of small steps:
1. Take a small batch of training panels and compute predictions.
2. Measure the error on that batch.
3. Work out which direction to nudge each weight to reduce the error (the gradient).
4. Nudge the weights.

**Adam** is the rule for step 4: how big a nudge each weight gets. It's gradient descent with two refinements (Lecture 7):
- **Momentum.** It keeps a running average of recent gradients, so steps follow the consistent direction instead of zig-zagging.
- **Per-weight step sizes.** It scales each weight's step by how large its gradients have been, so weights with big gradients take smaller steps and vice versa.

The learning rate (2e-3 for scratch, 1e-3 for fine-tuning) sets the overall size of the nudges. Adam knows nothing about when to stop.

## Early stopping: when to stop

Left alone, the loop would run for all 800 epochs (an epoch is one pass through the training panels). With 37 panels and about 10,000 weights, the network would eventually **memorise** those 37: training error near zero, but bad predictions on new panels. That's overfitting.

Early stopping prevents this by watching panels the network **isn't trained on**. The rule is in `MLP.fit` ([mlp.py:115-127](starter/mlp.py#L115-L127)):

1. **Before training,** split the n panels 75/25. At n = 50, that's **37 to train** on and **13 to watch**. Adam only ever sees the 37.
2. **After each epoch,** compute the MSE on the 13 watched panels.
3. **If it's the best so far,** save a copy of all the weights and note the epoch.
4. **If it hasn't improved for 60 epochs in a row** (the patience), stop.
5. **When training ends, restore the saved best weights**, not the final ones.
6. **Hard cap:** 800 epochs, even if it's still improving.

The error on the watched panels usually drops at first, while the network learns real patterns, then rises as it starts memorising the 37. Early stopping keeps the weights from around the bottom of that curve:

```
error on the 13
  │\
  │ \          ___/‾‾‾       ← memorising the 37: gets worse
  │  \___   __/
  │      \_/  ← best epoch: weights saved here
  │       ↑    ←── 60 epochs of no improvement ──→ stop, restore
  └──────────────────────────────── epochs
```

## Why "13 panels is a noisy judge"

The whole decision rests on the MSE of 13 panels, and that's a shaky number:
- **One unusual panel can dominate it.** For example, one of the badly delaminated negative-strength panels has a huge squared error and can swing the average on its own.
- **So the "best epoch" is partly chance.** The minimum might come from a lucky fluctuation, not from the point where the network truly generalises best. It could stop too early (barely trained, close to predicting the mean) or too late (already overfit).
- **A different 37/13 split gives a different stopping point.** That's another source of variation across draws.

At n = 1,000 the watched set is 250 panels, so the stopping decision is much more reliable. That's part of why the SDs should shrink as n grows.

Ridge uses the same 37/13 split for a similar purpose: choosing its penalty strength instead of a stopping epoch. Both are the same idea, using held-out panels to decide how much complexity to allow, and both are equally shaky at n = 50.

total variance ≈ variance from the draw + variance from the encoder

**Bracketing** means giving the crossover as a **range between two of the label counts you measured**, not as a single number.

## What it means

You only measured four points: n = 50, 100, 250 and 1,000. Suppose the table showed (made-up numbers):

| | n = 50 | n = 100 | n = 250 | n = 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frozen pre-trained | better | better | **better** | worse |
| From scratch | worse | worse | **worse** | better |

The ordering flipped somewhere between 250 and 1,000. You never measured 300, 500 or 800, so you don't know where in that range it happened. The honest statement is:

> "The crossover lies **between 250 and 1,000** labels."

That's a bracket. What you must **not** do is draw straight lines between the points, see where they cross, and announce "the crossover is at 437 labels." The rubric names that as a lost mark: *"Announcing a crossover to the decimal. Four label counts bracket a crossover. They do not locate one."*

## How the SDs widen the bracket

A flip only counts where the gap is bigger than the SDs. Suppose:
- At **n = 100**, frozen is clearly ahead (gap bigger than the SDs).
- At **n = 250**, they're within each other's SDs, so you can't tell them apart.
- At **n = 1,000**, scratch is clearly ahead.

Then the honest bracket is **"between 100 and 1,000."** You can't use n = 250 to narrow it, because at 250 the table doesn't know who's ahead. You could add "the two are indistinguishable at 250," which is useful information in itself.

This matters for the budget question in the memo. The bracket tells Kestrel the range of label counts where the best method changes.

## How B gets compared

Yes, it's built exactly like the other rows, using the **same 5 draws**. For each label count n:

```
for each draw r = 0..4:
    take the same n panels ridge (and every other method) gets for draw r
    "model" = the average strength of those n panels   (one number, e.g. 31.9 MPa)
    predict that number for all 1,000 dev panels → dev MSE
cell = mean ± SD of those 5 dev MSEs
```

You then compare cell to cell. For example, ridge at n = 50 (mean ± SD over 5 draws) against predicting the mean at n = 50 (mean ± SD over the same 5 draws).

A small terminology point: the mean prediction isn't **random**. Given the 50 panels, it's fully determined, since it's just their average. Its only randomness is **which 50 panels the draw picked**, so its SD should be small: the mean of 50 numbers doesn't move much from one sample to the next. That makes it a stable line to measure the incumbent against.

On check 1 and population drift: they're related, but not the same thing.

- **Drift checks** ask whether the *data* has changed: do new panels look different from the ones the model was trained on?
- **Check 1** asks whether *training worked*: did the fill-in-the-blanks game actually teach the encoder anything?

They're connected, though. Reconstruction error can be *used* as a drift detector. A pre-trained encoder rebuilds panels like the ones it trained on well, so if new panels suddenly rebuild badly, they're unlike the historical ones.
